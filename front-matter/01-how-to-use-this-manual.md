# How to Use This Manual

## The weekly rhythm

A laboratory period is three hours. That is enough time to take good data
*once*, and not enough time to take bad data twice. The schedule below is
built around that fact.

**Before the period (roughly one hour).** Read the experiment's Theory
section and answer the Pre-lab questions in your notebook. The pre-lab is not
busywork: in most of these experiments it produces the number you need in
order to know whether your first reading is sensible. A student who has not
computed the expected fringe spacing cannot tell a good alignment from a bad
one, and will spend the period taking data from a misaligned instrument.

**First twenty minutes.** Set up, and take a *deliberately crude* measurement
of the headline quantity. Compare it to your pre-lab estimate. If they
disagree by more than a factor of two, something is wrong with the apparatus
or with the estimate, and now — not at the end of the period — is when to find
out which.

**Next two hours.** Take the real data. Record it in the notebook as you go,
in a table you drew *before* you started filling it in. Photograph the
apparatus. Note the ambient conditions if they can matter.

**Last twenty minutes.** Do a quick fit before you leave. You have the
apparatus in front of you for another twenty minutes; you will not have it
next week. A fit that comes out obviously wrong is a reason to take ten more
minutes of data, and the whole point of fitting in the room is to give
yourself that chance.

**After the period.** Write the report.

## What you hand in

Two things, on different schedules:

**A notebook**, checked at the end of every period and graded four times a
semester. It is a contemporaneous record: what you did, what you read off the
instrument, what went wrong, and what you did about it. It is not a fair copy.
See [](#lab-notebook).

**A report**, one per experiment, due one week after the period. Reports use
the structure and the voice described in [](#lab-notebook) — the same one used
in the physics literature. Three of the fourteen reports each semester are
designated **full reports** (typically Weeks 4, 10, and 14); the rest are
**short reports** of two to three pages that omit the introduction and
literature discussion.

## Reading the notation

:::{list-table} Conventions used throughout
:header-rows: 1

* - Marker
  - Meaning
* - **[ ] Checkpoint**
  - Show this to the instructor before continuing, and tick the box. It is
    usually the point past which a misalignment becomes expensive to undo.
* - A **Danger** admonition
  - A hazard that can injure you: laser light, ionizing radiation, mains
    voltage, hot surfaces.
* - A **Warning** admonition
  - A hazard to the apparatus, or a step that quietly ruins the data.
* - A **Tip** admonition
  - A technique that makes the measurement work better.
* - `code`
  - Python you are expected to adapt, not to run unchanged.
:::

## Working in pairs

Experiments are done in pairs, and both partners must be able to explain every
part of the setup. A useful discipline: swap roles at the halfway point, so
that whoever has been at the keyboard goes to the optics bench and vice versa.
Data are shared; analysis and writing are individual.

## When something does not work

This is the normal condition of experimental physics, and handling it is part
of what the course teaches. In order:

1. **Check that the instrument is measuring what you think.** Disconnect the
   physics and see whether the reading goes where it should. A photodiode that
   reads the same with the laser blocked is not measuring the laser.
2. **Change one thing.** Note the change in the notebook before you make it.
3. **Estimate before you adjust.** If a fringe count is off by a factor of
   two, the likely cause is a factor of two in the geometry, not noise.
4. **Ask.** After you have written down what you tried.

A report that documents a measurement which failed, diagnoses why, and bounds
what could be concluded anyway will earn more credit than one that reports a
suspiciously perfect number with no error analysis.
